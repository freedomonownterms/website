AI can do your homework in seconds. That's exactly the problem: if it does the thinking, you don't learn, and the exam will show it. But used the right way, the same tool becomes something students have never had before: **a patient tutor, available at any hour, that never gets tired of your questions.**

This guide shows you how to use ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Copilot to learn *more*, not less. Every prompt is ready to copy. Replace the parts in **[brackets]** with your own details.

## The golden rule: make the AI ask, not answer

Research on learning consistently shows that we remember what we **work to recall**, not what we passively read. So the best way to study with AI is to make it question *you*.

The major assistants now have features built for exactly this:

- **ChatGPT: Study mode.** Guides you through problems with questions and hints instead of handing over answers.
- **Gemini: Guided Learning.** Breaks topics into steps and can add visuals, videos and quizzes.
- **Claude: Learning mode.** Uses a Socratic, question-led style, and can work with documents you've uploaded to a project.

Look for these options in the tool menu or when starting a new chat. Names and availability change, and some features depend on your plan or country, so check what your app offers. If you can't find one, you can get the same effect with a prompt:

> Act as my tutor for [subject]. Don't give me answers directly. Ask me questions, give hints when I'm stuck, and only explain fully after I've tried. Start by asking what I already know about [topic].

## 1. Get clear explanations at your level

Textbooks are written for everyone. AI can explain things for *you*:

> Explain [topic] like I'm a complete beginner, using a simple everyday example. Then explain it again at exam level, using the key terms I should write in my answers.

Still confused? Follow up with "Explain it a different way", "Give me an analogy" or "What's the most common misunderstanding about this?"

## 2. Test yourself constantly

Self-testing is one of the most effective study techniques there is. Let the AI quiz you:

> Quiz me on [chapter or topic] with 10 questions, mixing multiple-choice and short answers. Wait for my answers, then mark them and explain any mistakes.

Answer **without looking at your notes**. Getting things wrong now is how you avoid getting them wrong in the exam. For flashcards and longer quizzes, see our guide to [turning your notes into flashcards and quizzes with AI](/guides/turn-notes-into-flashcards-and-quizzes-with-ai/).

## 3. Use the "explain it back" technique

If you can teach it, you know it. After studying a topic, explain it to the AI:

> I'm going to explain [topic] in my own words. Check my explanation for mistakes, gaps and anything I've misunderstood. Don't rewrite it. Ask me questions about the weak parts. Here's my explanation: [type it out]

## 4. Solve problems step by step, then try one yourself

For maths, science, accounting or programming, don't ask for the answer. Ask for the *method*, then practise:

> Solve this problem step by step and explain why each step works. Then give me a similar problem to try on my own: [paste problem]

Better still, try first and ask for feedback:

> Here's my attempt at this problem. Don't give me the answer. Tell me where my reasoning goes wrong and give me a hint: [problem and your working]

## 5. Make a realistic study plan

> Create a day-by-day study plan for my [exam] on [date]. Subjects and chapters: [list]. I can study [hours] a day. Put my weak areas first ([list]) and include revision days and practice tests.

Ask it to adjust as you go: "I fell behind by two days, rework the plan."

## 6. Learn from past papers

> Here are questions from previous [exam] papers: [paste]. Find the most repeated topics and patterns, and tell me where to focus my preparation.

Then ask it to show you how a top-scoring answer to one of those questions would be structured, and write your own.

## 7. Get feedback on your writing, not a rewrite

> Give feedback on my [essay / assignment] about [topic]: structure, argument, clarity and grammar. Don't rewrite it for me. Tell me what to improve and why: [paste]

Making the changes yourself is how your writing actually improves.

## Where the line is

Every school and university has its own rules on AI, and they vary widely. **Read your course's policy and follow it.** As a general guide:

| Usually fine (check your rules) | Usually not allowed |
|---|---|
| Explaining concepts you don't understand | Submitting AI-written work as your own |
| Quizzing yourself and making study plans | Using AI during closed-book tests or exams |
| Feedback on drafts you wrote | Getting AI to complete graded homework |
| Brainstorming and organising ideas | Paraphrasing AI text to hide where it came from |

If you're not sure whether something is allowed, ask your teacher. It's a normal question now.

## Don't trust everything it says

AI can be **confidently wrong**: it sometimes invents facts, dates, formulas and even sources. To stay safe:

- **Check important facts** against your textbook, notes or a trusted source.
- **Never cite a source** the AI gives you without opening it and confirming it says what the AI claims.
- **Upload your own material.** Tools that answer from documents you provide, like NotebookLM or projects in Claude and ChatGPT, tend to stay closer to your syllabus.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is ChatGPT free for students?**
Yes, the free versions of ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot are good enough for everything in this guide. Some companies also offer student discounts on paid plans, which come and go, so check their websites.

**Will AI make me lazy?**
Only if you let it do the thinking. If you use it to question you, test you and give feedback, it makes you work *harder*, in a good way.

**Which AI is best for studying?**
Any major assistant works. The study features differ a little; see our roundup of the [best free AI tools for students](/guides/best-free-ai-tools-for-students/).
