Recruiters now see a lot of cover letters that start with "I am thrilled to apply for this exciting opportunity" and go on to describe the candidate as "passionate", "dynamic" and "results-driven". Those letters were written by AI, and they all sound the same.

The problem isn't using AI. It's using AI **lazily**. A cover letter written *with* AI, from your real details and edited in your own voice, can be better than one you'd write alone. Here's how.

All prompts work with ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot. Replace the parts in **[brackets]** with your details.

## Why most AI cover letters fail

AI defaults to safe, generic language because that's what it has seen most often. Without specific details, it fills the gaps with:

- **Over-the-top enthusiasm:** "thrilled", "excited", "eager to leverage"
- **Buzzwords:** "dynamic", "synergy", "proven track record", "fast-paced environment"
- **Claims with no evidence:** "excellent communication skills" without an example
- **Nothing about the company** beyond its name

Every one of these problems comes from a lack of input. Fix the input and you fix the letter.

## Step 1: Collect three things

Before you open the AI, gather:

1. **The job description**, pasted in full
2. **Your resume**, or a list of your relevant experience
3. **One specific reason you want this job at this company.** Something you read on their website, a product you use, a recent news story, a value they talk about, or someone you know who works there.

Number 3 is what makes a letter feel human, and it's the one thing AI can't invent for you.

## Step 2: Use a prompt with built-in rules

Paste this, with your details:

> Write a cover letter for the [job title] role at [company], under 250 words. Use my resume and the job description below. Open with why I'm interested in this specific company: [your specific reason]. Connect 2–3 of my strengths to their needs, with a real example for each. End with a clear next step. Rules: no clichés, no words like "thrilled", "passionate", "dynamic" or "leverage", and write in plain, confident English. Resume: [paste] Job description: [paste]

Three things make this prompt work:

- **A word limit.** Short letters get read. Long ones get skimmed.
- **A specific reason** that only you could give.
- **Banned words.** Telling the AI what *not* to say removes most of the "AI voice".

## Step 3: Make it sound like you

Even a good draft won't sound exactly like you. Give the AI a sample of your own writing, such as an email you've sent or a paragraph about yourself:

> Rewrite this cover letter so it sounds like me. Here is something I wrote: [paste a sample of your writing]. Keep it under 250 words.

## Step 4: Edit by hand (the most important step)

Read the letter out loud. Anything you wouldn't actually say to a person, change. Then check:

- [ ] Does the first sentence say something only *I* could say?
- [ ] Is there at least one concrete example, with a number or result if possible?
- [ ] Is the company's name spelled correctly, and is it the right company?
- [ ] Have I deleted every cliché?
- [ ] Is every claim true and something I can talk about in an interview?

## A quick before and after

**A typical AI opening:**
"I am thrilled to apply for the exciting Marketing Assistant opportunity at your esteemed organisation. As a passionate and dynamic professional, I am eager to leverage my skills."

**After using the method above:**
"I've used Brightleaf's budgeting app for two years, and your recent campaign helping students track spending is the reason I'm applying. Last year I ran Instagram for my college's finance club and grew it from 300 to 1,900 followers with a similar money-tips series."

The second version is specific, believable and memorable, and it's the same length.

## Shorter alternatives

Not every application needs a full letter. AI can help with these too:

**A short application email:**

> Write a short, polite email applying for [job title] at [company] with my resume attached. Include a clear subject line. Keep it under 120 words.

**A follow-up after applying:**

> Write a polite follow-up email about my application for [job title] at [company], sent on [date]. Keep it brief and add one new reason I'm a good fit: [reason].

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Sending the first draft.** It's a draft. Always edit.
2. **Reusing the same letter.** If you could send it to any company by changing the name, it's not specific enough.
3. **Inventing reasons or achievements.** "I've always admired your company" means nothing. A real, specific reason means everything.
4. **Pasting private information.** Leave out phone numbers, addresses and ID numbers when using AI tools.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do recruiters still read cover letters?**
Not always, but when they do, a weak one can hurt and a strong one can set you apart, especially for smaller companies, career changes and competitive roles. If an application asks for one, write one.

**Will an AI detector flag my cover letter?**
AI detectors are unreliable, and most employers don't use them on cover letters. What recruiters *do* notice is generic writing. A letter built from your real details and edited in your own voice won't read as AI-written.

**How long should a cover letter be?**
Usually 200–300 words: three or four short paragraphs. Shorter is fine if it's specific.
