Career gaps are far more common than most people think. People take time out to study, care for family, recover from illness, move country, start a business, or simply because finding the next job took longer than planned.

A gap only hurts when it looks like you're hiding it. Explained briefly and confidently, it rarely stops a good candidate. This guide shows you how to do that on your resume and in interviews, with free AI prompts to help you find the words.

## The golden rule: honest, short, forward-looking

A good gap explanation does three things:

1. **Says what happened**, in one plain sentence. No long story is needed.
2. **Shows anything useful you did**, like a course, freelance work, volunteering or a skill you learned.
3. **Moves to the future**, explaining why you're ready now and why this role fits.

You never need to share private medical or family details. "I took time out to care for a family member, who's now well" is complete and professional.

## Step 1: Decide where the gap goes on your resume

You have two simple options:

- **A one-line entry in your experience section**, with dates. For example: "Career break: family caregiving (2024–2025)".
- **No separate entry**, with the gap covered briefly in your summary or cover letter instead.

If you did anything during the gap, such as a certificate, freelance projects or volunteering, list it with dates. That turns "time off" into "time used well".

## Step 3: Prepare for the interview question

Interviewers often ask about gaps, and a calm, prepared answer works far better than a nervous one. Practise with these free prompts:

- [Tricky HR questions](/prompts/interviews/tricky-hr-questions/) gives you honest, confident answers to awkward questions, gaps included.
- ["Tell me about yourself"](/prompts/interviews/tell-me-about-yourself/) helps you build an introduction that mentions the gap briefly and moves on.

A strong answer takes about 20 seconds. For example: *"I took eight months out to care for my father after surgery. He's fully recovered now. During that time I completed a Google Data Analytics certificate, and I'm excited to put it to work in a role like this."*

## Examples for common situations

| Reason | Resume line | Interview angle |
|---|---|---|
| Studying or a course | "Full-time study: [course name] (dates)" | What you learned and how it fits the job |
| Caring for family | "Career break: family caregiving (dates)" | Organisation and responsibility, and that you're ready now |
| Health | "Career break: personal health (dates), now fully fit for work" | Keep it brief. You don't owe details |
| Job search after a layoff | Usually no separate line | Brief and factual, then what you did meanwhile |
| Freelancing or a side business | List it as experience | Clients, results and skills used |
| Moving country | "Relocation to [country] (dates)" | Adapting, plus new languages or skills |

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Stretching dates to hide the gap.** Background checks can catch it, and it damages trust.
- **Over-explaining or apologising.** One calm sentence is enough.
- **Being negative** about a former employer or a hard time.
- **Copying AI text without reading it.** Check that every word is true.

## The bottom line

A career gap is part of your story, not the end of it. Explain it in a sentence, show anything you learned, and point to the future. The free [Explain a career gap](/prompts/resumes/explain-a-career-gap/) prompt gives you a first draft in seconds, and you make it yours.
