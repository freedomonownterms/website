Scams aren't new. What's new is how convincing they've become. With AI, criminals can **copy a person's voice from a short clip**, **make videos of celebrities saying things they never said**, and **write messages with perfect spelling and grammar** in any language.

The good news: the defences are simple, and they work no matter how good the technology gets. This guide covers the most common AI-powered scams, the warning signs, and the habits that keep you and your family safe.

## The most common AI scams

### 1. The fake emergency call (voice cloning)
You get a call from someone who sounds exactly like your child, parent or friend. They're in trouble: an accident, an arrest, a stolen wallet abroad. They need money urgently, and they beg you not to tell anyone.

Scammers can clone a voice from audio posted online, such as social media videos or voice messages. The voice may sound real, but **the story is the scam**.

### 2. Deepfake celebrity and expert videos
Videos or ads showing a famous business leader, celebrity, doctor or news presenter promoting an investment, cryptocurrency, miracle cure or "government scheme". The face and voice look and sound real, but they've been generated with AI.

### 3. Fake bosses and officials
A message, call or even a video call from someone who appears to be your manager, a company director, a bank, the police or a government office, asking you to transfer money, buy gift cards, share a code or "verify" your details.

### 4. Perfectly written phishing
Fake emails and texts from banks, delivery companies, streaming services or tax offices. Bad spelling used to be a giveaway. With AI, scam messages can now read as professionally as the real thing.

### 5. Romance and friendship scams
A new online friend or match with attractive photos, which may be AI-generated, who builds trust over weeks before asking for money or pushing an "investment opportunity".

### 6. Fake job offers
Offers of easy, well-paid remote work that end with requests for a "registration fee", training costs, your bank details or ID documents.

## The warning signs

Whatever the technology, scams almost always share the same pattern:

- **Urgency:** "You must act now", "within the hour", "before it's too late"
- **Secrecy:** "Don't tell anyone", "keep this between us"
- **Strong emotion:** fear, panic, excitement, love or greed
- **Unusual payment:** gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, payment apps to a stranger, or "safe accounts"
- **Requests for codes or passwords:** one-time passcodes, PINs, login details
- **Too good to be true:** guaranteed returns, prizes you didn't enter, jobs with no interview

**If you notice any of these, stop.** You can't always tell whether a voice or video is fake, but you can always notice the pressure.

## Six habits that protect you

**1. Agree a family safe word.**
Choose a word or phrase that only your family knows and that never appears online. If someone calls claiming to be a relative in trouble, ask for it. A scammer with a cloned voice won't know it.

**2. Hang up and call back.**
If a call or message claims to be from someone you know, your bank or an official body, end the conversation and contact them **using a number you already have**, such as one saved in your phone or printed on your card or the official website. Never use a number or link the caller gives you.

**3. Slow down.**
Real emergencies and real banks can wait five minutes while you check. Scammers can't, which is exactly why they rush you.

**4. Never share codes.**
No genuine bank, company or government office will ask you for a one-time passcode, PIN or password. Anyone who asks is a scammer.

**5. Verify investment and "official" videos at the source.**
If a video shows a celebrity or official promoting something, check their verified accounts or official website. If it isn't there, assume it's fake.

**6. Be careful what you share publicly.**
The more video and audio of you there is online, the easier it is to copy your voice. Consider making social media accounts private and being selective about what you post.

## Spotting fake images and video

Detection tools exist, but **none are reliable enough to depend on**, and AI keeps improving. Small glitches can be clues: strange hands or teeth, blurry edges around the face, lip movements that don't quite match the words, unnatural blinking, or lighting that doesn't fit. But a clean-looking video can still be fake.

Better checks:
- **Search for the story.** Is it reported by reputable news outlets?
- **Do a reverse image search** (for example with Google Lens) to see where a photo first appeared.
- **Check the account.** Is it new, unverified or full of similar posts?

## Use AI to check a suspicious message

AI can help protect you too. Paste a suspicious message (with your personal details removed) into ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Copilot:

> Here is a message I received. Check it for signs of a scam: urgency, unusual payment requests, requests for codes or personal details, suspicious links or sender addresses, and anything that doesn't add up. Tell me what to verify before I respond: [paste the message]

Treat the answer as a second opinion, not a guarantee. When in doubt, contact the organisation directly.

## If you've been targeted

1. **Stop all contact** and don't send any more money or information.
2. **Contact your bank immediately** if you've paid or shared financial details. Acting fast can sometimes stop or recover a payment.
3. **Change your passwords** and turn on two-factor authentication if you shared login details.
4. **Report it** to your country's fraud reporting service or police. For example: in the United States, [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov); in India, [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in) or the 1930 helpline.
5. **Warn friends and family**, especially if the scam used your name or voice.
6. **Don't be ashamed.** These scams are designed by professionals to fool smart people. Reporting helps protect others.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much audio does someone need to clone my voice?**
It varies, but modern tools can produce a convincing imitation from a fairly short recording, which is why the safe word and call-back habits matter more than trying to hide your voice entirely.

**Can AI detection apps tell me if a call is fake?**
Some apps claim to, but none are reliable enough to rely on. Verification habits work every time.

**Are AI scams only a problem for older people?**
No. Everyone is targeted, including students (fake jobs and scholarships), professionals (fake bosses and invoices) and investors (deepfake endorsements).
