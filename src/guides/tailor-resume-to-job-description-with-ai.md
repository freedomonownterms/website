Sending the same resume to every job is the most common reason good candidates don't hear back. A resume tailored to the job, using the employer's own language and putting your most relevant experience first, gets noticed more often.

Tailoring used to take an hour per application. With AI it takes about fifteen minutes. This guide shows you how, and clears up what "beating the ATS" really means.

All prompts work with ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot. Replace the parts in **[brackets]** with your details.

## What is an ATS, really?

An **applicant tracking system (ATS)** is software that companies use to collect and manage job applications. Well-known examples include Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS and Taleo.

You'll often read that "75% of resumes are rejected by robots before a human sees them." That claim gets repeated a lot, but there's no solid evidence behind it. In practice, an ATS mostly does two things that matter to you:

1. **It reads (parses) your resume** into fields like job titles, dates and skills. Fancy layouts can confuse it.
2. **It lets recruiters search and filter** applicants, often by keywords, skills or job titles.

So the goals are simple: make your resume **easy for software to read**, and make sure it **contains the words a recruiter would search for**, but only where they're true.

## Step 1: Pull out what the employer wants

Paste the job description and ask:

> Analyse this job description. List the top 10 skills and keywords the employer wants, in order of importance. Separate must-have requirements from nice-to-haves, and note any job titles or tools mentioned. Job description: [paste]

Read the results. This is the vocabulary your resume should speak.

## Step 2: Find your gaps

Now compare it with your resume:

> Here is a job description and my resume. Show me which of the top skills and keywords my resume already covers, which ones are missing, and which ones I have but described in different words. Job description: [paste] Resume: [paste]

The third category is the most useful. You might have written "made dashboards in Excel" when the job says "data visualisation". Same skill, different words, and a recruiter's keyword search might miss you.

## Step 3: Rewrite, using only the truth

Ask the AI to adapt your resume:

> Rewrite my Summary and Skills sections and reorder my bullet points to match this job description, using the employer's wording where it accurately describes my experience. Only use things that are true about me. If a requirement isn't covered by my experience, list it separately instead of adding it. Job description: [paste] Resume: [paste]

That last instruction matters. It keeps the AI from slipping in skills you don't have, and gives you a list of real gaps to address in your cover letter or work on for the future.

## Step 4: Check the format

Ask the AI to review your layout:

> Review my resume for applicant tracking systems (ATS). Point out formatting problems (tables, columns, icons, text in headers), non-standard section names, and keywords I should add for a [job title] role. Resume: [paste]

Then follow these formatting rules, which work for both software and humans:

- **One column.** Two-column designs often get read in the wrong order.
- **Standard headings:** Summary, Experience, Education, Skills, Projects.
- **No tables, text boxes, icons or images** for important information.
- **Contact details in the main body,** not in the page header or footer.
- **Common fonts** such as Arial, Calibri or Georgia.
- **Clear dates** in a consistent format, like "Jun 2024 – Present".
- **Save as PDF or Word,** whichever the employer asks for. If they don't specify, a text-based PDF is usually fine.

**A quick test:** copy all the text from your PDF and paste it into a plain text editor. If it comes out jumbled or out of order, software may struggle with it too.

## Step 5: Do a final human check

Ask the AI for one last review from a recruiter's point of view:

> Act as a recruiter hiring for this role. Score my tailored resume out of 10 for this job description and list the 3 changes that would improve it most. Job description: [paste] Resume: [paste]

Then read it yourself. Does it still sound like you? Is every line true? Could you talk about every bullet point in an interview?

## Don't do this

Some tips you'll find online are tricks that backfire:

- **Hiding keywords in white text.** Modern systems show the text as plain text, and recruiters see it. It looks dishonest.
- **Copying the job description into your resume.** Obvious, and it doesn't say anything about you.
- **Stuffing in keywords for skills you don't have.** You may get the interview, but you won't get through it.
- **Overloading with every possible keyword.** A resume should still read naturally to a human.

## A 15-minute routine for every application

1. Paste the job description and get the top keywords (2 min)
2. Compare with your base resume and find gaps (3 min)
3. Rewrite the Summary and Skills, and reorder bullets (5 min)
4. Read through, fix anything untrue or unnatural (4 min)
5. Save as "Firstname-Lastname-Resume-Company.pdf" (1 min)

Keep a **master resume** with everything you've ever done, and create a tailored copy for each application.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many keywords should I include?**
There's no magic number. Cover the main skills and tools from the job ad that you genuinely have, using their wording, in context, such as in a bullet point that shows how you used them.

**Can I use one resume for similar jobs?**
For very similar roles, yes, with small changes to the summary and skills. For different types of roles, tailor more heavily.

**Do I need a paid "ATS checker" tool?**
No. A free AI assistant and the formatting rules above cover what most people need.
