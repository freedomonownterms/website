Millions of people now use AI to help with resumes, cover letters and interview prep. Many still feel slightly guilty about it, and wonder whether employers will see it as cheating.

The short answer: **using AI as a writing and practice tool is generally fine.** Using it to misrepresent yourself, or to get help during a live test or interview, is not. Here's where the line is, and why.

## Why using AI isn't cheating

People have always had help with job applications. Friends proofread resumes, career centres review cover letters, and paid writers produce whole applications. Spell-checkers and grammar tools have been around for decades.

AI is a faster, more available version of that help. What employers want to know from your application hasn't changed:

- **Do you have the skills and experience for the job?**
- **Can you communicate clearly?**
- **Are you genuinely interested in this role?**

If AI helps you show the truth about those things more clearly, it's doing what a good mentor or editor would do.

## What's generally fine

| Use | Why it's fine |
|---|---|
| Improving the wording of your resume | You're presenting real experience more clearly |
| Drafting a cover letter from your real details, then editing it | The content and reasons are yours |
| Tailoring your resume to a job description | You're highlighting relevant truth, in the employer's language |
| Proofreading and fixing grammar | The same as a spell-checker or a friend's review |
| Mock interviews and practice questions | It's preparation, like rehearsing with a friend |
| Researching a company before an interview | You're doing your homework |
| Understanding a job offer or contract | You're making an informed decision |

## What crosses the line

**1. Inventing experience, skills or results.**
AI will happily write "increased sales by 40%" if you let it. If it isn't true, don't use it. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, references get checked, and background checks are common. Being caught in a lie can end an application, or a job you've already started.

**2. Using AI during a live interview or assessment.**
Getting real-time answers from AI during a video interview, coding test or online assessment is cheating, just like having someone whisper answers to you. Many employers now watch for it, and it can lead to disqualification.

**3. Ignoring an employer's instructions.**
Some employers ask candidates not to use AI for certain parts of the application, such as a written question about why you want the job. If they ask, respect it. They want to see your own thinking, and ignoring the instruction tells them something about you.

**4. Submitting work samples you didn't create.**
If a role asks for a writing sample, portfolio or take-home task, it should reflect your own ability. If you used AI as a tool, and that's allowed, be ready to say how.

## What recruiters actually notice

Recruiters rarely mind *that* you used AI. What puts them off is what lazy AI use produces:

- **Generic writing** that could belong to anyone
- **Buzzwords and over-the-top enthusiasm** ("thrilled to leverage my dynamic skill set")
- **Claims without evidence** ("excellent communicator", with no example)
- **A resume that doesn't match the person** they meet in the interview

## Should you say you used AI?

You usually don't need to announce that you used AI to polish your resume, any more than you'd mention a spell-checker. But:

- If an application **asks** whether you used AI, answer honestly.
- If an interviewer asks how you prepared, it's fine to say you practised with an AI tool. Many will see that as resourceful.
- If you used AI for a work sample and the employer allows it, be open about how you used it.

## The privacy side

Whatever you paste into an AI tool may be stored by the provider. Before pasting your resume or an offer letter:

- **Remove** your phone number, home address, ID numbers and bank details
- **Don't paste** confidential information from a current or previous employer
- **Check the settings** of your AI tool. Many let you turn off chat history or model training.

## A simple rule of thumb

Before using AI for any part of an application, ask yourself:

> "Would I be comfortable if the employer saw exactly how I used AI here?"

If the answer is yes, because you used it to organise, polish and practise, you're fine. If the answer is no, because it invented something or did the work they wanted to see from you, don't do it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can employers detect AI-written resumes?**
AI detection tools are unreliable and can wrongly flag human writing. Employers are much more likely to notice generic, buzzword-heavy writing than to run a detector. A resume built on your real experience and edited by you reads like any strong resume.

**Is it OK to use AI for a government or university application?**
Check the rules first. Some institutions have explicit policies on AI use in applications, essays or statements of purpose. Follow them carefully.

**Will using AI make all applications look the same?**
Only if people use it lazily. Your experience, your specific reasons and your voice are what make an application stand out, and AI can't supply those.
